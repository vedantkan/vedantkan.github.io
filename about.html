How I got here

My path into statistics wasn't a straight line. I trained as an electronics and communication engineer, moved into information science at the University of Pittsburgh, and then spent three years as a data scientist in Dr. Jan Claassen's Disorders of Consciousness Lab at Columbia. There, we used EEG to look for signs of hidden consciousness in patients who appear completely unresponsive after a brain injury.

It's the most meaningful work I've done. Seeing our research reach the bedside and play a part in patients' recovery was deeply satisfying, and it's why biostatistics is still close to my heart.

Over time, I noticed the same pattern: real clinical data kept raising questions that standard methods weren't built to answer, and I could see the gaps more clearly than I could fill them. I wanted to grow from someone who applies methods into someone who asks his own research questions and develops the tools to answer them. That's what brought me to a Ph.D. in Statistics at Penn State.

Outside the clinic, my other love is sport. I follow Real Madrid and Formula 1, and I'm a huge MS Dhoni fan. Formula 1 is where the statistician in me gets most curious. Every car streams an enormous amount of live data, and I keep coming back to one question: how do some drivers look after their tyres so much better than others over the same number of laps? I hope to build research like this in sports analytics alongside my work in biostatistics.

And when I'm not thinking about data, I'm usually cooking, eating or out on a run.

If you work on any of this, in biostatistics, sport or both, I'd love to hear from you at vkansara at psu dot edu.
